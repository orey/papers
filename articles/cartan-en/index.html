Einstein, Cartan, and Differential Geometry#

I sometimes research things about the structure of the universe. For example, I've never believed in the existence of black holes. I see the center of galaxies more as a kind of crystal, with stars blocking each other in their respective gravitational fields. The center of galaxies would therefore be a kind of material with the properties of a very slightly ductile solid, due to rotation (by the way, what makes galaxies spin, inertia?). Moving away from the rotating center, the gravitational forces pass through clusters of stars, hence that stringy/filamentous appearance.

The subject of dark matter also makes me pensive. When you have a theory that predicts we can't see 85% of the matter in the universe, you probably need to change the theory! Let's start by assuming the photon has mass and redo the calculations!

This weekend, I read a few articles by Cartan on differential geometry and affinely connected manifolds, notably On manifolds with affine connection and the theory of generalized relativity: here, here and here.

I think I've understood two main concepts from these rather fascinating readings.

  1. Differential geometry studies the properties of non-Euclidean spaces in the neighborhood of points by considering local transformations that can be likened to Euclidean transformations. These transformations, according to Cartan, break down into three fundamental transformations (each represented by a tensor): Torsion (translation) and curvature (which has a rotational component and a homothety/dilation component). Cartan is interested in the invariants of these transformations, invariants which, ultimately, characterize the space in question.

  2. These transformations make it possible to explain how to progressively pass from spaces with different macroscopic properties depending on the location. For example, if the point considered is in a vacuum, it won't be subject to the same gravitational fields as in the vicinity of a rotating star.

Manifolds thus allow for the characterization of these non-homogeneous spaces by "stitching together" local homogeneous spaces via transformations (the connections).

What's interesting in this field of mathematics is that the tools being developed are perfectly suited for physicists who want to "stitch theories together," especially when these theories are potentially not at the same scale. Because what differential geometry allows is to work on transformations of points in tiny neighborhoods and thus to study the transitions of non-Euclidean spaces through successions of more familiar transformations on tangent spaces.

Einstein had used, for the theory of general relativity, a Riemannian space in which torsion was considered to be zero. Cartan, in his letters to Einstein, shared the subject of including a torsion parameter, forgotten (?) by Einstein.

"Without going into more detail on this subject"? What the? Cartan indicates a method for unifying gravitation and electromagnetism, in 1923, and his work remains a dead letter... It's incomprehensible.

Cartan's geometric description of non-Euclidean spaces is particularly interesting: by distinguishing the three components of local transformation of a non-Euclidean space in the vicinity of a point (translation, rotation, and homothety, and thus "torsion" and "curvature"), Cartan characterizes spaces where different rules can apply in different places. In fact, depending on the location, the different components will take on more or less significant values while remaining homogeneous at the microscopic level. The decomposition of these transformations into invariants allows for describing the structure of our universe, a structure in which the laws we know are applicable, within a given neighborhood.

Thus, philosophically, it's natural that what I experience in the Euclidean reference frame of the solar system isn't entirely applicable at the center of the galaxy, nor in the void between two galaxies, even though we are in a "continuous" universe, whether geographically or in terms of scale.

Finally, if the model with torsion is a generalization of general relativity that includes electromagnetism, why are physicists still searching for the unification of forces?
